If you're reading this in late July and your 2026 security plan looks identical to the one you presented in December, one of two things is true: you have an extraordinarily stable environment, or you haven't been paying close enough attention. For most organizations, the first six months of any year produce enough change — in threats, in business priorities, in technology rollouts, in team composition — that the back half of the year benefits significantly from a structured plan adjustment rather than autopilot execution.

The mid-year review is a discipline that separates programs that achieve their stated outcomes from programs that show up in December surprised by how much didn't get done. It doesn't have to be elaborate. A few hours of honest assessment, a handful of conversations with stakeholders, and a willingness to change course where needed produces dramatically better results than continuing to push against assumptions that no longer hold.

## Start with What You Said You'd Do

The first step in any mid-year review is reading your own plan from the start of the year. This sounds obvious, and it's astonishing how many security teams skip it. The plan articulated specific priorities, objectives, and success criteria. Some of those will have been met, some will be on track, some will be off track, and some will have been quietly abandoned. The review needs to engage with all four categories honestly.

A useful structure is to walk through each major initiative from the original plan and assign one of four statuses: on track and worth continuing as scoped; on track but should be expanded or accelerated; off track and recoverable with intervention; off track and should be deprioritized or canceled. The fourth category is the one most teams resist, because admitting that an initiative isn't going to deliver feels like failure. It's not. Deprioritizing work that isn't producing results frees capacity for work that will. The failure isn't recognizing the situation — it's continuing to push on something that isn't working.

The same exercise applies to assumptions. The 2026 plan was built on assumptions about threat priorities, regulatory direction, business growth, technology roadmaps, and team capacity. For each major assumption, ask whether it still holds. We've covered the assumption-testing discipline in the context of [evidence-based threat modeling](/blog/security-planning-for-2026-part1-evidence-based-threat-modeling/), and the same approach applies to broader strategic assumptions. When assumptions change, the work built on them needs to be revisited.

## Reassess the Threat Picture

Six months into 2026, the threat landscape looks different than it did at the end of 2025. Specific threat actor groups have shifted tactics, new vulnerabilities have surfaced, and incident patterns have evolved. Your threat-informed priorities should reflect that evolution rather than the snapshot you took during planning.

A useful exercise is to list the top five threat scenarios driving the 2026 plan and ask, for each one, whether the scenario is more or less likely now than it was at the start of the year, and whether new scenarios have emerged that warrant top-five status. Inputs to this exercise include your own incident data from the first half, sector-specific intelligence, regulatory enforcement actions, public incident reports, and the conference research that surfaces emerging techniques months ahead of mainstream coverage.

This reassessment often produces small adjustments rather than dramatic shifts. The point isn't to rebuild the threat model from scratch — it's to ensure the program is responding to current threats rather than threats that mattered most six months ago. When the assessment does produce a significant change, that change deserves explicit attention in the back-half plan rather than being absorbed implicitly into existing work.

## Audit Capacity Honestly

Capacity is where mid-year reviews most consistently surface uncomfortable truths. The plan assumed a certain headcount, a certain level of operational load, and a certain amount of unplanned work. The reality almost always differs. Vacancies that took longer to fill than planned have eaten into project capacity. Incident response work has consumed time that wasn't budgeted. New systems went live with security work that wasn't on the radar in December. Compliance audits showed up with timelines that weren't anticipated.

Honest capacity assessment looks at how much project time the team has actually delivered against the plan and projects forward based on that observed throughput rather than the optimistic assumptions in the original plan. If the first half delivered 60% of planned project capacity, the back half is unlikely to deliver more without specific intervention. The question becomes which projects to prioritize within that real capacity, not how to magically deliver the original portfolio.

This is also the moment to evaluate where outside help would meaningfully accelerate the program. Consulting engagements, managed services, contract resources, and partner relationships all have real costs but can produce specific outcomes that internal capacity won't reach by year-end. Mid-year is late enough to know what's actually behind and early enough for outside investment to matter.

## Listen to Stakeholders

The mid-year review is an opportunity to check in with the executives, business unit leaders, and technology partners whose support the security program depends on. The check-ins aren't formal status reports — they're conversations about whether the program is delivering what they need, where it's adding friction, and what's coming in the back half that the security team should know about.

These conversations consistently surface issues that wouldn't reach the security team through normal channels. Business units that have lost confidence in a specific control or process. Technology partners with roadmaps the security team hadn't heard about. Executives who are starting to worry about a risk the team has been deprioritizing. The conversations cost a few hours and produce intelligence that shapes the back-half plan substantially.

The conversations also matter for relationship reasons. Stakeholders who get a deliberate mid-year check-in feel engaged with the program. Stakeholders who only hear from the security team during incidents and budget cycles feel managed. Over time, the difference compounds in how much support the program receives when it needs it.

## Refresh the Metrics

Metrics that were defined in December may or may not still be the right ones. Some will have proven useful and worth keeping. Some will have produced data that didn't drive any decisions and can be retired. Some will have surfaced new questions that warrant new measures. The mid-year review is the right time to refresh the measurement set rather than waiting for the next planning cycle.

The criterion for keeping a metric is simple: did anyone make a decision based on this metric in the first half of the year? If yes, keep it. If no, either change how you're using it or stop measuring it. Metrics that don't drive decisions consume reporting time and dilute attention from the metrics that do. We're aggressive about this because the cost of vanity metrics compounds — they create the appearance of measurement without the substance, and they crowd out better measures.

New metrics often emerge from the threat reassessment and stakeholder conversations. If a new risk has become prominent, the program needs a way to measure its progress against that risk. If a stakeholder concern has surfaced, the program needs visibility into the dynamic the stakeholder is worried about. Adding metrics is easy; choosing the right ones requires connecting them explicitly to decisions the program intends to make.

## Build the Back-Half Plan

The output of the mid-year review is a revised plan for the remainder of the year. The plan acknowledges what the original plan got right, what it got wrong, what's been completed, what's in flight, and what's been added or removed. It's grounded in the capacity the team has actually been delivering, the threats that actually matter now, and the stakeholder priorities that have actually emerged.

The revised plan should be communicated explicitly rather than absorbed silently into ongoing work. Executives who saw the original plan in December should see the revision in July, with the changes called out and the rationale articulated. This isn't about asking permission to adjust — it's about maintaining a shared understanding of what the security program is doing and why. Programs that adjust quietly create the impression of drift; programs that adjust visibly demonstrate adaptive management. The difference shows up later, in the credibility the program brings to next year's planning conversation.
